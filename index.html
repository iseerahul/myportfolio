Teardown: Gemini Live in Indian languages

Gemini Live speaks 9 Indian languages. Does it speak the way India talks?

I work on a voice platform that makes ~400K AI calls a day to buyers across India, and my research is about metrics that look good while hiding failure. This teardown sits right where those two meet.

What Google got right

Gemini Live supports Hindi plus Bengali, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu, and it’s free on Android. On India’s largest real-world speech benchmark, Voice of India, Gemini models performed close to India-built systems while several global models lagged far behind. The foundation is strong.

The gap: India doesn’t speak one language per conversation

  1. Code-switching. Gemini Live follows the language you set, and Google’s rollout allowed up to two languages on a device. A Mumbai user moves between Marathi, Hindi and English in a single sentence, and a family phone may be shared across three languages.
  2. Averages hide the users who get left out. On Voice of India, error rates sit around 5–6% for Hindi and Bengali but 15–20% for Tamil, Telugu and Malayalam. Dialects like Bhojpuri hit 20–30%, against under 10% for standard Hindi. “Supports Hindi” can mean very different things to someone in Mumbai and someone in North Bihar.
  3. Turn-taking breaks first. In AI calling, I analyse connect, latency and drop-off funnels. When the agent is unsure, it hesitates; the user talks over it; overlapping audio makes recognition worse. One misheard place name turns into three wasted turns.

Who this hurts most

First-time voice AI users outside metro Hindi and English. They’re exactly the people for whom voice is easier than typing, and one bad conversation can be enough to make them give up.

My proposal: “Speak naturally” mode

  • Segment-level language detection. Detect language per phrase rather than per user, so one turn can mix any supported Indian language with English. The setting becomes a hint, not a wall.
  • Confidence-gated repair. When confidence on a key entity (a place, a name, a number) is low, Gemini asks one short confirmation in the user’s dominant language instead of guessing. Live captions underline the low-confidence word so the user can tap and fix it.
  • Ship gates per dialect cluster. Internally, a language only counts as “launched” when its worst dialect cluster passes the bar, not the average.

Whether Live runs a cascaded pipeline or a native audio model, the same decisions exist. The proposal changes three of them.

Evaluation would use orthographically informed WER (OI-WER), which accepts valid spelling variants so code-mixed speech isn’t penalised just for spelling, sliced by language, dialect cluster and audio quality.

Metrics

North star

Successful non-English Live conversations per weekly user: the user got what they asked for without repeating themselves.

Quality gap

Error rate of the worst dialect cluster minus standard Hindi. The goal is shrinking the gap, not the average.

Repair cost

Confirmation questions per conversation. Too many and the fix becomes the annoyance.

Talk-over rate

Turns where the user interrupts the agent, a direct signal of latency and hesitation.

Early abandon

Sessions ended in the first 30 seconds, split by language.

Trade-offs I’d accept, and ones I wouldn’t

  • A confirmation adds a turn and some latency. Worth it for entities that change the answer (places, numbers), not for small talk.
  • Per-segment language detection can flip-flop on short words. I’d smooth it across a few seconds and bias towards the user’s recent languages.
  • I wouldn’t collect extra dialect audio without clear consent. Evaluation can rely on consented benchmark data like Voice of India.

How I’d validate it in 2 weeks

Run 30 Live sessions with 10 people who naturally mix Marathi, Hindi and English. Log every failure by type (misrecognised entity, wrong language, talk-over), then compare against single-language sessions. If code-switched sessions fail far more often, the problem is real and measurable.

Teardown: Google Maps for “what’s on tonight”

Maps knows 35 million places in India. It doesn’t know what’s happening tonight.

I’m building Bambai, a map for finding places, events and hangouts in Mumbai. That work made one gap in Google Maps obvious to me.

What Google got right

Ask Maps launched in March 2026 in the US and India. It answers conversational questions about places, like finding spots with good veg appetizers where you can watch today’s match, and it’s grounded in Google’s data on 35 million places in India. For “where should I go?”, it’s already excellent.

The gap: places are permanent, plans are not

A lot of what people actually plan around is time-bound: an open mic at 8:30, a weekend flea market, a match screening, a pop-up. That information mostly lives in Instagram posts, printed posters and WhatsApp forwards. Maps’ index is built around places, so a place profile can say a café exists, but not that something worth going to starts there in two hours.

hypothesis: try asking Ask Maps “what’s on tonight in Bandra” and see what comes back

Who this hurts most

Young people in big Indian cities deciding what to do in the next few hours, and small venues and organisers who have no budget for promotion beyond a poster and an Instagram post.

My proposal: “Happening” as a first-class object

  • Events become time-bound entities attached to a place, with a start time, end time, price and language, and they expire automatically.
  • Three ways in: owners post them from Business Profile; users point Lens at a poster and contribute it in one tap; ticketing partners send structured feeds.
  • Ask Maps gets a time dimension: “tonight”, “this weekend” and “in the next 2 hours” become real filters, and answers show when something starts and how sure Maps is.

The hard parts are the unglamorous ones. Indian posters mix scripts and write dates as DD/MM, so extraction needs per-field confidence and a hold rule for ambiguous dates. Deduplication clusters by place ID, time window and title similarity so five posters for one gig become one listing. Expiry is the safety net: a stale event is worse than no event.

Metrics

North star

Plans made: navigations to an event’s place that start within the event’s time window after an Ask Maps answer.

Stale-event rate

Users who arrive and report nothing was there. The single biggest trust risk.

Date precision

Share of extracted events with the correct date and time, by script and language.

Duplicate rate

Same event shown more than once in an answer.

Coverage

Share of “tonight”-type questions that return at least one trusted event.

Trade-offs

  • Freshness against trust. Crowd-scanned posters are fast but unverified. I’d show them with a clear “seen on posters” label and rank owner-verified events higher.
  • Cold start outside metros. Tier-2 cities have fewer posts. Lens scans by users matter most there, so I’d launch in 2–3 metros and measure contribution rates before expanding.
  • Monetisation can wait. Promoted events are an obvious ad slot, but mixing paid and organic before trust is earned would undermine the feature.

How I’d validate it in 2 weeks

Use Bambai’s 20-user closed beta: log every search and tag it as place-based or time-based. Then photograph 50 event posters around Bandra and Dadar and check how many of those events are findable on Maps today. If a large share of real plans are time-based and missing, the gap is proven.

Teardown: Business Profile and the missed call

Google’s AI calls shops for users. India’s problem is the shop that never picks up.

At Square Yards I shipped Unknown Listener, which caught inbound calls our AI agent was silently missing. It added 1,000+ leads to the pipeline in 2 months and 2 closed deals. Missed calls are lost customers, and that lesson applies far beyond real estate.

What Google got right

Ask for Me lets Google’s AI call local businesses on a user’s behalf to check prices and availability, then send back a summary. Every call announces that it’s automated, businesses can opt out from their Business Profile, and call quotas stop shops getting flooded. It started with nail salons and auto shops in the US, and reports from Google I/O 2026 say it’s expanding to home repair. It’s a careful, well-designed rollout.

The gap: in India, the bottleneck is the other end of the phone

Ask for Me solves “I don’t want to make the call.” In India, a huge amount of local commerce already happens by phone: customers find a shop on Maps and tap call. The weak point is the shop: one person at the counter, a busy evening rush, a phone in a drawer. Every unanswered call is a customer who quietly goes to the next listing. Ask for Me is also US-only and English-only today, while these calls happen in Hindi, Marathi, Tamil and more.

at Square Yards, the leads hiding in missed calls were real buyers

Who this hurts most

Small shop owners who can’t afford a receptionist, and customers who give up after one unanswered call.

My proposal: a missed-call assistant in Business Profile

  • One-tap setup. The owner turns on forwarding for busy and unanswered calls from the Business Profile app, which walks them through their phone’s standard call-forwarding setting.
  • Gemini answers in the caller’s language, using only what’s in the profile: hours, address, listed prices and products. Anything else gets “The owner will call you back.”
  • The owner gets a callback card with the caller’s name, what they wanted, their language and urgency, plus a one-tap call-back button.

How it works under the hood

Conditional forwardingbusy or no answer goes to a Google number
→
Real-time voice agentdetects language, discloses it’s AI
→
Groundinganswers only from profile fields and owner FAQs
→
Lead cardneed, language, urgency, callback time

Learning looprepeated questions suggest profile fields to fill

The most important design choice is the grounding rule. A voice agent that invents a price or promises stock can cost the owner money, so any answer not backed by a profile field becomes a callback, never a guess. The learning loop turns the most common unanswered question into a prompt for the owner to add that information, which also makes the listing better for Search and Ask for Me.

Metrics

North star

Missed calls recovered: forwarded calls that end in an owner callback, order or visit.

Ungrounded-answer rate

Answers not backed by a profile field. Target: zero, audited weekly.

Early hang-up

Callers who hang up within 10 seconds of hearing the AI greeting, by language.

Callback speed

Time from lead card to owner call-back.

Owner retention

Owners who keep forwarding on after 30 days, and opt-out reasons.

Trade-offs

  • Caller trust. Some callers will hang up on any AI. Disclosure stays non-negotiable; I’d test greeting scripts per language to reduce hang-ups.
  • Setup friction. Call forwarding is unfamiliar to many owners, so guided setup and a test call matter more than the AI itself.
  • Cost per call. I’d start with high-intent categories (food, repairs, healthcare clinics) where one recovered customer clearly pays for many calls.

How I’d validate it in 2 weeks

Call 30 Maps-listed shops near me during evening peak and record how many pick up. Then interview 5 owners about how often they miss calls and what they lose. If the answer rate is low and owners can name lost orders, the problem is real before a single line of code.
